01 / Understand access

What digital accessibility means

Digital accessibility means designing websites, applications, documents, media, and online services so people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate, and interact with them independently.

Perceive

Receive the information

Text can be read. Images have alternatives. Video has captions. Meaning is not conveyed only through color or sound.

Example: a menu is real text, not a photo of a menu.

Understand

Know what to do

Instructions are clear. Navigation is predictable. Forms identify errors and explain how to correct them.

Example: an error says which field to fix and how.

Navigate + interact

Complete the task

Controls work with a keyboard and assistive technology. Focus is visible. Time limits and motion do not create barriers.

Example: booking works without ever touching a mouse.
“

The power of the Web is in its universality. Access by everyone regardless of disability is an essential aspect.

Tim Berners-Lee, inventor of the World Wide Web

01 / Understand access

Access is part of every important task

For many people, the website is the front door. A barrier can interrupt a task that affects health, education, work, money, participation, or belonging.

Finda menu, schedule, policy, or phone number
Decidewhether a service, event, or opportunity fits
Actbook, apply, register, buy, donate, or ask for help
Confirmknow the action succeeded and what happens next

A barrier

The appointment form cannot be used without a mouse.

The customer cannot select a time or submit the request.

The result: a lost booking, a longer phone queue, and a person who plans around your website instead of with it.

An accessible path

The form works by keyboard and gives a clear confirmation.

The customer completes the same task independently.

The result: the task is done in one visit, and the confirmation says exactly what happens next.

Accessibility improves access, clarity, resilience, and trust. It also reduces the cost of correcting the same barrier after it has spread across pages, documents, and campaigns.

01 / Understand access

People see and hear digital content differently

Someone may read by sound, braille, magnification, or customized colors.

People who are blind may use a screen reader. People with low vision may zoom, magnify, enlarge text, or replace colors and fonts.

What helps
Real text alternatives, strong contrast, named controls, and layouts that hold together when enlarged.
Common barriers
Missing text alternatives, low contrast, unlabeled controls, and layouts that break when enlarged.

Someone may depend on captions, transcripts, visual alerts, or sign language.

Deaf and hard-of-hearing people may use text alternatives to speech and meaningful sound.

What helps
Accurate captions, complete transcripts, visual versions of audio alerts, and text-based ways to get support.
Common barriers
Uncaptioned video, audio-only instructions, inaccurate auto-captions, and alerts communicated only through sound.
Remember: Assistive technology can only communicate the structure and alternatives that the content provides.

01 / Understand access

People move, process, and communicate differently

01

Motor access

Someone may navigate with a keyboard, switch, voice control, eye tracking, head pointer, or adapted input device.

Watch for mouse-only controls, tiny targets, drag-only actions, short time limits, and keyboard traps.
02

Cognitive + learning access

Someone may benefit from clear language, visible instructions, consistent layouts, and the ability to pause or take more time.

Watch for vague instructions, dense text, unexpected changes, lost form data, and distracting motion.
03

Speech access

Someone may not be able to use voice-based support, phone systems, or voice identity checks.

Provide another effective way to communicate or verify identity.
04

Temporary + situational access

A broken arm, fatigue, glare, a noisy room, slow connection, or an eye or ear injury can change what someone needs.

Flexible design supports people whose needs change across a day or a lifetime.
One takeaway: Design for flexibility. The same page is read by ear, by touch, by keyboard, and at very different sizes.

01 / Understand access

The four foundations: POUR

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) rest on four principles—Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust, remembered as POUR. Use them as a shared vocabulary, not as four isolated checklists.

P

Perceivable

People can receive the information.

Alt text, captions, contrast, adaptable structure.
O

Operable

People can navigate and use the interface.

Keyboard access, visible focus, enough time, motion controls.
U

Understandable

Information and interactions make sense.

Clear instructions, predictable patterns, useful errors.
R

Robust

Content works across technologies.

Semantic structure, accurate names, roles, states, and values.

About conformance

WCAG 2.2 includes testable success criteria at Levels A, AA, and AAA. Passing a few checks does not establish conformance for an entire site. Use this guide to begin; use the complete standard and qualified evaluation when assessing conformance.

01 / Understand access

A useful test has more than one layer

No single method finds every barrier. Combine three layers, and treat each one as evidence rather than a verdict.

Fast signal

Automated checks

Find some code-based issues at scale: contrast failures, missing attributes, empty labels, and repeated patterns.

Cannot decide: whether alt text is useful, focus order is logical, or a task is understandable.
Human judgment

Manual checks

Review keyboard use, zoom, focus, forms, headings, media, and instructions—and complete real tasks.

Cannot replace: the lived experience of disabled users.
Real experience

Evaluation with disabled people

Reveal barriers, workarounds, and confusing patterns that technical checks may miss.

Complements: standards-based evaluation; it does not replace it.
Better evidenceautomated signal+manual review+disabled user feedback

Color contrast: make important text easy to read

Same message, two presentations

Important updateRegistration closes Friday.
Fails about 1.6:1 contrast
Important updateRegistration closes Friday.
Passes about 17.9:1 contrast

Low contrast can make text difficult to distinguish, especially over images, in faint interface states, or on a screen affected by glare.

Check these combinations

  • Body text and its background
  • Text placed over photographs
  • Links, buttons, and form borders
  • Hover, focus, selected, error, and disabled states
WCAG 2.2 Level AA contrast reference
Generally 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Review the complete criterion before making a conformance claim.
4.5:1normal text
3:1large text
3:1icons + parts of controls
Copy into a report: “The foreground and background colors may not provide sufficient contrast in the normal and interactive states. Please test every state and update the palette while preserving a visible focus indicator.”

Alternative text communicates the information or purpose of an image when the image is not seen.

Two attempts, one image

Before writing alternative text, identify the job the image is doing. Start at the top and use the first description that fits.

Functional image

Does it perform an action?

Example: a magnifying-glass image is the button that opens search.

Name the action

03 / Watch and listen

Captions and transcripts preserve the message

Start with auto-captions; finish with human review. Automatic output can be useful, but it often misses names, accents, punctuation, and specialized language.

Captions

Synchronized text that plays with the video, representing speech and meaningful sound as they happen.

  • Review names and technical terms
  • Identify speakers when needed
  • Include meaningful sounds
  • Use readable punctuation and timing
  • Keep captions clear of essential visuals

Transcripts

A separate text version of the full content. It supports search, translation, quiet settings, and people who prefer reading.

A transcript complements captions; it does not always replace synchronized captions in video.

03 / Watch and listen

Make visual action, controls, and motion understandable

AD

Describe essential visuals

If a video communicates necessary information visually that is not explained in the existing audio, add audio description or integrate the information into the narration.

Example: say which control is selected, not only “complete these steps.”
▶

Make controls operable

People should be able to play, pause, adjust volume, enable captions, stop autoplaying audio, and find controls when zoomed.

Test controls by keyboard and with assistive technology.
❙❙

Control motion

Avoid unnecessary flashing. Give people a way to pause significant animation, scrolling, blinking, or automatic updates.

Respect reduced-motion preferences where possible.
Autoplay rule of thumb: nothing should start moving or making sound on its own without an obvious, keyboard-reachable way to stop it.
One content review, three questionsCan the message be understood without hearing?Can essential visuals be understood without seeing?Can the experience be controlled?

Visible focus, logical order, and no traps

Keyboard users watch the focus indicator the way mouse users watch the pointer. Follow it through this five-minute check: put the mouse aside, press Tab, and complete one real task.

ServicesBook an appointmentContact
→
Appointment request

Name

Focus enters the dialog, follows the form, and returns to the trigger when closed.

01 Visible

You can always tell which element will receive the next keypress.

Check: an outline appears on every link, button, and field.

02 Logical

Focus generally follows the reading and visual order, including inside dialogs.

Check: tab through once; focus never jumps somewhere surprising.

03 Escapable

Focus does not become trapped in a menu, widget, embedded frame, or media player.

Check: Esc or Tab always leads out of whatever was opened.
Custom control warning: A styled <div> does not automatically behave like a button. Prefer native controls; if a custom component is necessary, provide the correct name, role, state, keyboard behavior, and focus management.
Copy into a report: “While tabbing through the page, the focus indicator disappears after the [element], and the focus order skips the [control]. Please restore a visible indicator and a logical order.”

04 / Navigate and complete tasks

Forms: label the task before asking for input

Appointment request

Required fields are marked with an asterisk. Nothing is submitted from this printed example.

A form should explain what belongs in each field, what is required, and what will happen next.

  • Permanent labels remain visible while the person types.
  • Instructions appear before they are needed.
  • Required status is communicated in text, not color alone.
  • Formats include a useful example when necessary.
  • Buttons describe the result: “Request appointment,” not “Submit.”
Placeholder text is a hint, not a label. It disappears, may have low contrast, and can be confused with entered data.
Copy into a report: “The [field] has no visible label, and required fields are marked only by color. Please add permanent labels and a text indication of required status.”

Write links and instructions that make sense alone

Weak

Click here

Select the green button on the right.

→

More useful

Download the patient registration form

Select Continue to payment.

Meaningful links

Link text should identify its destination or action when read without the surrounding sentence. Avoid identical link text for different destinations.

Predictable navigation

Repeated navigation, search, help, and controls should remain reasonably consistent across pages.

Plain, direct language

  • Use familiar words when possible.
  • Explain necessary technical terms.
  • Present steps in the order completed.
  • Expand acronyms the first time.
  • Avoid relying only on color, shape, sound, or position.
Instead of“Click here” · “Learn more” · “Read this”
Try“Download the form” · “Start your application” · “Contact the clinic”
Copy into a report: “Several links on the page read only ‘click here’ or ‘learn more.’ Please rewrite each link so it names its destination or action.”
Quick test: Scan only the headings, links, and buttons. Can you predict the page structure and the result of each action?

05 / Publish and purchase

Accessible documents begin in the source

01

Structure the source

Use heading styles, real lists, table headers, descriptive links, and meaningful document titles.

Where: the Styles panel, list buttons, and table header row settings.
02

Add alternatives

Describe meaningful images, preserve readable contrast, and establish a logical reading order.

Where: the alt-text pane and your tool’s color and order settings.
03

Export carefully

Use an accessible export path. Do not print to PDF when it removes document structure.

Where: “Save as PDF” with tagging on—not “Print to PDF.”
04

Test the final file

Run the checker, inspect tags and order, and navigate the exported file by keyboard—not only the source.

Where: the built-in accessibility checker, then a real read-through.

Common barrier

A scanned menu or form is only an image.

It may be unreadable to a screen reader and difficult to enlarge or reflow.

Better path

Publish the information as accessible web text.

If a document is needed, provide a properly structured version and verify the exported file.

The guide you are reading follows the same path: structured source, described images, tagged export, and a tested final file.

Accessibility is a workflow, not one person’s cleanup job

Every role touches access. When each role owns its part of the workflow, barriers stop reappearing faster than they can be fixed.

LeadersSet scope, goals, ownership, and resources.Approve policy. Review progress. Remove blockers.
ContentWrite clear copy and provide meaningful alternatives.Use headings, links, alt text, captions, and accessible documents.
DesignCreate readable, flexible, predictable patterns.Check contrast, focus, zoom, motion, errors, and component states.
DevelopmentImplement semantic, operable interfaces.Use native controls, names, roles, states, keyboard behavior, and testing.
QA + usersTest tasks and verify corrections.Combine automated, manual, and disabled-user evaluation.
ProcurementRequire evidence and track vendor barriers.Test before purchase and maintain an escalation path.

Make feedback visible: publish a contact method for accessibility problems, acknowledge reports, explain next steps, and communicate when the issue is resolved.

Start with priority tasks, then rank the barriers

Begin with the pages and processes people rely on most—not the pages that are easiest to test.

  • Navigation + contact
  • Service information
  • Scheduling
  • Registration
  • Checkout + payment
  • Applications
  • Documents
  • Support

Test each one in the configuration your audience actually uses—on a phone, zoomed in, and with assistive technology.

Critical
Fix nowPrevents completion of an essential task.
High
NextCreates serious difficulty or blocks important information.
Medium
SoonCreates confusion, delay, or additional effort.

Priority is not permission to ignore

Fix simple barriers when you can, even if they are not critical. Rank work so essential tasks are restored quickly while every known barrier remains visible and owned.
